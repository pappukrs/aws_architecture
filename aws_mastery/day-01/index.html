IAM in Depth

You already write policies and attach roles. Today is about what AWS does with them. Every request passes the same checks in the same order, and six kinds of policy take part. You will see why one bucket policy behaves differently for a caller in the same account and a caller in another account, what happens inside AssumeRole, and how to read an AccessDenied message so that you open the right policy first.

How it works inside

Two policy types grant, four only limit

  • Identity-based and resource-based policies are the only ones that give a permission.
  • SCP, RCP, permissions boundary and session policy never give anything. An Allow in them only means “not blocked here”.
  • An explicit Deny in any of the six wins over every Allow.
  • In one account, the usual result is: (identity policy or resource policy) and SCP and boundary and session policy. The next card lists the cases where this is not true.
  • Policies are read on every request. Edit a role's policy and sessions that already exist change with it.

Resource policy: who it names matters

Same account, caller is a role session:

  • Names the account (:root or the account ID): it hands the decision to IAM in that account. The identity policy must still allow.
  • Names the role ARN: the grant is added to the role. No identity policy is needed, but the boundary and the session policy still limit it.
  • Names the session ARN (assumed-role/role/session-name): a direct grant. An implicit deny in the identity policy, the boundary or the session policy does not matter.
  • IAM user ARN: also direct. The user's boundary does not limit it.
  • Principal: "*" with a condition on aws:PrincipalArn is not limited by the boundary or the session policy either.

Two resource policies are stricter: a role trust policy and a KMS key policy must allow the principal themselves. An identity policy alone is never enough for them. Day 18 covers key policies.

Cross-account: two evaluations

  • AWS evaluates the request once in the caller's account and once in the resource's account. Both must return Allow.
  • Caller's account: identity policy, limited by its SCPs, boundary and session policy.
  • Resource's account: the resource-based policy, limited by its RCPs.
  • So the same-account shortcut is gone. A bucket policy that names a role in another account does nothing until that role's identity policy also allows the action.
  • If the resource type has no resource-based policy, the only way in is a role in the resource's account (panel B).

Permissions boundary

  • A managed policy set on one user or one role as its maximum. It grants nothing.
  • Its use is delegation. Let a team create roles, but only with a condition such as "iam:PermissionsBoundary": "arn:…:policy/team-boundary" on iam:CreateRole, iam:PutRolePolicy and iam:AttachRolePolicy. The team can then never create a role stronger than the boundary.
  • Also deny iam:DeleteRolePermissionsBoundary and edits to the boundary policy itself, or the limit can be removed.
  • It does not limit a direct grant from a resource policy (see the card before).
  • A resource policy with NotPrincipal and Deny always denies a principal that has a boundary. Use a condition on aws:PrincipalArn instead.

STS and role sessions

  • AssumeRole returns an access key ID, a secret key, a session token and an expiry time. The principal becomes arn:aws:sts::ACCOUNT:assumed-role/ROLE/SESSION-NAME.
  • The session has the role's permissions only. The caller's own permissions are not added.
  • A session policy passed to AssumeRole narrows one session. Use it to hand out one role with a different scope per tenant or per job.
  • Role chaining is using a role session to assume another role. A chained session lasts one hour at most.
  • Session tags and the source identity travel with the session and appear in CloudTrail. Tags marked transitive and the source identity survive chaining.
  • You cannot delete temporary credentials. To cut off a stolen session you deny by issue time (see the failure table).

Trust policy details

  • When you save a trust policy whose Principal is a role or user ARN, IAM stores the unique ID (AROA…, AIDA…) in its place. Delete and recreate that role and the trust is broken, even with the same name.
  • arn:aws:iam::A:root means account A, not its root user. Account A's admins then decide who in A may call sts:AssumeRole.
  • A Principal cannot hold a partial wildcard such as role/ci-*. Name the account and add a condition: ArnLike on aws:PrincipalArn.
  • For a role used by an AWS service the principal is a service, for example lambda.amazonaws.com.
  • Giving a role to a service is a separate permission, iam:PassRole, on the person who configures the service. Without a tight Resource on it, anyone who can create a Lambda function can use your admin role.

The confused deputy

  • A deputy is something with more access than the party that asks it to act. It is confused when it uses that access for the wrong party.
  • Across accounts: a vendor assumes roles in many customers' accounts. A customer gives the vendor your role ARN. The fix is sts:ExternalId in your trust policy, with an ID the vendor fixes per customer.
  • Across services: you allow a service principal, such as cloudtrail.amazonaws.com, to write to your bucket. Anyone can configure that service in their own account to write to your bucket. The fix is aws:SourceArn or aws:SourceAccount in the resource policy, or aws:SourceOrgID for a whole organisation.
  • The role ARN and the bucket name are not secrets. Design as if the attacker knows them.

Condition keys to know

  • aws:PrincipalOrgID: the caller's account is in organisation o-…. With Principal: "*" it shares a resource with the whole organisation without a list of accounts.
  • aws:PrincipalArn: the ARN of the user or role. For a session it is the role ARN, not the session ARN.
  • aws:PrincipalTag/key: a tag on the user or role, or a session tag.
  • aws:ResourceTag/key: a tag on the resource. aws:RequestTag/key and aws:TagKeys: tags sent in the request itself.
  • aws:SourceArn, aws:SourceAccount: present when an AWS service calls on behalf of one of its resources. They are not present on a call by one of your roles.
  • A key that is missing from the request makes StringEquals false and StringNotEquals true. Check this before you write a Deny with a negated operator.

ABAC with tags

  • One policy for many teams: allow the action when aws:ResourceTag/team equals ${aws:PrincipalTag/team}. A new team needs new tags, not a new policy.
  • The principal's tags come from the role, or from session tags set by the identity provider at sign-in. A session tag overrides a role tag with the same key.
  • The tag is now the permission. Control who may set it: restrict tagging actions with aws:RequestTag and aws:TagKeys, or a team can re-tag another team's resource and take it.
  • Check each service first. Not every action supports authorisation by resource tag.

IAM Access Analyzer

  • External access findings: it reads resource policies and reports each resource that a principal outside your account or organisation can reach. It reasons about the policy; it does not wait for traffic.
  • Unused access findings: roles, access keys and permissions that nobody has used in a period you choose.
  • Policy validation: checks a policy document for errors and risky patterns before you save it.
  • Custom policy checks: API calls for a pipeline, such as “does this change grant any new access?” and “does this policy allow s3:DeleteBucket?”.
  • Policy generation: writes a policy from the CloudTrail activity of a role.

IAM Identity Center

  • The beginner track showed what it is for. Inside, a permission set is a template. For each account you assign it to, Identity Center creates an IAM role named AWSReservedSSO_name_suffix.
  • A person signs in once, picks an account and a permission set, and gets a session of that role. There is no IAM user and no long-lived key.
  • A resource policy should not put that role in Principal. Name the account and add ArnLike on aws:PrincipalArn with a wildcard for the suffix.
  • Everything on this page still applies: the role is a normal role, and SCPs and boundaries limit it like any other.

A global service with a slow edge

  • IAM is eventually consistent. A new role, policy or key can take some seconds to be visible everywhere. Code that creates a role and uses it at once must retry.
  • Do not create or change IAM resources in a path that must work during an incident. Create them ahead of time.
  • Use a Regional STS endpoint (sts.region.amazonaws.com), so that getting credentials does not depend on another Region.
  • STS has a request quota. Cache credentials until they are close to expiry. Do not call AssumeRole once per request.

Hands-on lab

You will see four of the seven checks deny a request, and read the policy type in each error. One account is enough. Use an admin role or user in a sandbox account, not the root user, in bash or zsh. Cost: IAM and STS are free. The bucket stays empty, and the handful of S3 list requests round to zero on the bill.

  1. Set up. A bucket, and a role that trusts your own account but only with an external ID. 
    export AWS_PAGER=""
ACCOUNT=$(aws sts get-caller-identity --query Account --output text)
BUCKET=m01-lab-$ACCOUNT
ROLE=arn:aws:iam::$ACCOUNT:role/m01-lab
aws s3 mb s3://$BUCKET

cat > trust.json <<EOF
{"Version":"2012-10-17","Statement":[{"Effect":"Allow",
  "Principal":{"AWS":"arn:aws:iam::$ACCOUNT:root"},
  "Action":"sts:AssumeRole",
  "Condition":{"StringEquals":{"sts:ExternalId":"m01-demo"}}}]}
EOF
aws iam create-role --role-name m01-lab --assume-role-policy-document file://trust.json
sleep 10   # IAM is eventually consistent
  2. The trust policy decides. Without the external ID the call is denied, even for an admin. A trust policy must allow the caller itself. 
    aws sts assume-role --role-arn $ROLE --role-session-name lab        # AccessDenied

login() {   # assume the role; extra arguments are passed to assume-role
  read AK SK ST < <(aws sts assume-role --role-arn $ROLE --role-session-name lab \
    --external-id m01-demo "$@" \
    --query 'Credentials.[AccessKeyId,SecretAccessKey,SessionToken]' --output text)
}
as_role() { AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID=$AK AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY=$SK AWS_SESSION_TOKEN=$ST "$@"; }
list() { as_role aws s3api list-objects-v2 --bucket $BUCKET --query KeyCount; }

login
as_role aws sts get-caller-identity --query Arn   # ...:assumed-role/m01-lab/lab
  3. Check 5, identity policy. The role has no policy yet. Read the end of the error. 
    list   # AccessDenied ... because no identity-based policy allows the s3:ListBucket action
  4. Check 4, the same-account grant. Name the role in the bucket policy. The role still has no identity policy, and the call now works with the same credentials. 
    grant() {   # $1 = the principal the bucket policy names
  aws s3api put-bucket-policy --bucket $BUCKET --policy "{\"Version\":\"2012-10-17\",\"Statement\":[{\"Effect\":\"Allow\",\"Principal\":{\"AWS\":\"$1\"},\"Action\":\"s3:ListBucket\",\"Resource\":\"arn:aws:s3:::$BUCKET\"}]}"
}
grant $ROLE
sleep 10
list   # 0  (the bucket is empty; the call is allowed)
  5. Check 6, permissions boundary. Set a boundary that allows only SQS reads. The grant to the role ARN is now cut. Then grant to the session ARN and it works again: a direct grant to the session is not limited by the boundary. 
    aws iam put-role-permissions-boundary --role-name m01-lab \
  --permissions-boundary arn:aws:iam::aws:policy/AmazonSQSReadOnlyAccess
sleep 10
list   # AccessDenied ... because no permissions boundary allows the s3:ListBucket action

grant arn:aws:sts::$ACCOUNT:assumed-role/m01-lab/lab
sleep 10
list   # 0
  6. Check 7, session policy. Remove the boundary and the bucket policy. Give the role a normal identity policy, then take a session that carries a narrower session policy. 
    aws iam delete-role-permissions-boundary --role-name m01-lab
aws s3api delete-bucket-policy --bucket $BUCKET
aws iam put-role-policy --role-name m01-lab --policy-name list --policy-document \
  "{\"Version\":\"2012-10-17\",\"Statement\":[{\"Effect\":\"Allow\",\"Action\":\"s3:ListBucket\",\"Resource\":\"arn:aws:s3:::$BUCKET\"}]}"
sleep 10
list   # 0  (identity policy allows)

login --policy '{"Version":"2012-10-17","Statement":[{"Effect":"Allow","Action":"s3:GetBucketLocation","Resource":"*"}]}'
list   # AccessDenied ... because no session policy allows the s3:ListBucket action
login  # a session without a session policy
list   # 0
  7. Ask the tools. The simulator evaluates the role's identity policies without making the call. Access Analyzer validates a policy document; this one should return a security warning about iam:PassRole on every resource. 
    aws iam simulate-principal-policy --policy-source-arn $ROLE \
  --action-names s3:ListBucket s3:DeleteBucket --resource-arns arn:aws:s3:::$BUCKET \
  --query 'EvaluationResults[].[EvalActionName,EvalDecision]' --output text
# s3:ListBucket    allowed
# s3:DeleteBucket  implicitDeny

aws accessanalyzer validate-policy --policy-type IDENTITY_POLICY \
  --policy-document '{"Version":"2012-10-17","Statement":[{"Effect":"Allow","Action":"iam:PassRole","Resource":"*"}]}' \
  --query 'findings[].[findingType,issueCode]' --output text
  8. Clean up. 
    aws iam delete-role-policy --role-name m01-lab --policy-name list
aws iam delete-role --role-name m01-lab
aws s3 rb s3://$BUCKET
rm trust.json
unset AK SK ST

If a result looks one step behind, wait a few seconds and run list again. The change has not reached every IAM endpoint yet. Some services do not put the policy type in the error; S3 does for requests in the same account.

Interview questions

A request is denied. In what order do you check SCP, boundary, identity policy and resource policy?

First read the error: it usually names the policy type and says whether the deny is explicit or implicit. If it does not, follow the evaluation order. One: look for an explicit Deny in all of them, because it ends the evaluation wherever it is. Two: the SCPs on the path from the root to the account, since without an Allow there nothing else matters. Three: the resource policy. Across accounts it must allow; in the same account it may be the whole grant. Four: the identity policy. Five: the permissions boundary, and after it the session policy if the caller is a role session. In each one, check the conditions and the exact resource ARN, not only the action.

Same account. A role has no identity policy for s3:GetObject, but the bucket policy allows that role. Does the read work? What changes if the role has a permissions boundary that does not include S3?

It works. In one account an Allow in either the identity policy or the resource policy is enough. With the boundary it depends on what the bucket policy names. If it names the role ARN, the boundary limits the grant and the read is denied. If it names the session ARN, the grant goes directly to the session and an implicit deny in the boundary does not apply. An explicit Deny in the boundary would block both.

The same bucket policy, but now the role is in another account. Why does the read fail?

A cross-account request is evaluated twice, once in each account, and both must allow. The bucket policy is the Allow of the bucket's account. The caller's account has said nothing yet: the role needs an identity policy that allows s3:GetObject on that bucket, inside its own SCPs and boundary. The reason is ownership. An account's admins decide what their principals may do, and another account's policy cannot decide that for them.

What is the confused deputy problem, and what exactly does an external ID do?

A vendor's account can assume roles in many customers' accounts. If the vendor's product assumes whatever role ARN a customer enters, customer X can enter the ARN of customer Y's role and read Y's data through the vendor. The vendor is the deputy. With an external ID, the vendor creates one ID per customer and sends that customer's ID on every AssumeRole call it makes for them. Y's trust policy requires Y's ID in sts:ExternalId, so a call made for X carries X's ID and fails. The ID is not a secret and must be chosen by the vendor, not by the customer.

A trust policy has "Principal": {"AWS": "arn:aws:iam::111111111111:root"}. Who can assume the role?

Not only the root user. It means the whole account 111111111111, by delegation: any user or role there whose identity policy allows sts:AssumeRole on this role. You have handed the choice to that account's administrators. To narrow it, name one role, or keep the account and add a condition on aws:PrincipalArn, an external ID or aws:PrincipalOrgID.

Permissions boundary and SCP both set a maximum. When do you use which?

An SCP is set by the organisation on whole accounts and binds every principal in them, the account's own admins included. Use it for rules with no exceptions inside the account: Regions, protecting the audit trail. A boundary is set inside an account on single users or roles. Use it to delegate: developers may create roles for their workloads, on the condition that each new role carries the boundary, so they cannot create anything stronger than the limit you chose. Neither grants a permission.

What is role chaining, and what breaks because of it?

Using the credentials of one role session to assume a second role. The chained session can last one hour at most, whatever the second role's maximum session duration is, and asking for more makes the call fail. Long jobs behind two hops therefore need automatic refresh. The original identity is also harder to trace, so set a source identity or transitive session tags, which carry through the chain into CloudTrail.

Temporary credentials of a production role were posted in a public chat. What do you do?

They cannot be deleted. Revoke the role's active sessions: this attaches a policy to the role that denies all actions when aws:TokenIssueTime is before now. The stolen session is dead, and workloads get new sessions and continue. Then look in CloudTrail for what the session did, by its access key ID or session name, and fix how it leaked. Deleting the role would also work, but it breaks the workload and every trust policy that names the role.

You want one policy for all teams with ABAC. How does it work, and what can go wrong?

The policy allows an action when the resource's tag equals the principal's tag, for example aws:ResourceTag/team and ${aws:PrincipalTag/team}. Principal tags come from the role or from session tags set by the identity provider. What goes wrong: someone who may change tags can move a resource into their own team, so tagging must be restricted with aws:RequestTag and aws:TagKeys; a role that may pass any session tag can claim any team, so sts:TagSession needs conditions too; untagged resources are reachable by nobody, or by everybody if the policy was written with a negated condition; and not every service supports tag conditions on every action.

A central S3 bucket must accept logs from every account in the organisation, and from an AWS service. How do you write the bucket policy?

Two statements. For your own accounts: Principal: "*" with a condition aws:PrincipalOrgID equal to the organisation ID. New accounts are covered without an edit, and nobody outside the organisation matches. For the service: its service principal, with aws:SourceArn or aws:SourceAccount, or aws:SourceOrgID for the whole organisation, so that the same service configured in a stranger's account cannot write to your bucket. Each sending role still needs an identity policy, because this is cross-account.
