Networking at Scale

Yesterday you followed one packet through one VPC. A company has fifty VPCs, a data centre and a partner whose address range is the same as yours. Today you connect them. The centre of the page is the transit gateway and one question: which route table does a packet read, and who filled it? Then you add Direct Connect with a VPN behind it, DNS in both directions, and the three ways to share a service without sharing a network.

How it works inside

Where peering stops

  • A peering connection joins exactly two VPCs. It is not transitive: if A peers with B and B with C, A cannot reach C (architecture Day 22).
  • A full mesh of n VPCs needs n(n−1)/2 connections. Ten VPCs need 45, and every one needs routes on both sides.
  • Nothing can pass through a peered VPC: not a VPN, not Direct Connect, not a NAT gateway.
  • It still has a place. It has no hourly charge, no data-processing charge and no bandwidth limit. For two VPCs that exchange a lot of data, it is the cheapest path.

Transit gateway: association and propagation

  • A transit gateway is a router for one Region. Everything connects to it through an attachment: a VPC, a VPN, a Direct Connect gateway, a peering connection to another transit gateway, or a Connect attachment for SD-WAN appliances.
  • Association answers “which route table does traffic arriving from this attachment read?”. An attachment has exactly one.
  • Propagation answers “which route tables learn this attachment's routes?”. An attachment can propagate into many tables, or into none.
  • The two are independent. An attachment can read a table it does not write to.
  • By default a transit gateway has one route table, and every attachment is associated with it and propagates into it. That is a full mesh. Turn both defaults off when you create the gateway.

Segmentation

  • Isolation on a transit gateway is the absence of a route. There is no deny rule to write.
  • The usual pattern: one route table per environment, and one hub table for shared services and hybrid links. Spokes propagate into the hub table. The hub propagates into every spoke table. Spokes never propagate into each other's tables.
  • A static 0.0.0.0/0 for central egress matches every address, including the other environments. Add blackhole routes for the ranges that must stay apart. A blackhole is a static route that drops.
  • Routing is checked per direction. A route in one table and none in the other gives one-way delivery, not isolation.
  • Give each environment its own address block, for example 10.1.0.0/16 for prod and 10.2.0.0/16 for dev. Then one blackhole covers every VPC that team will ever create.

The attachment inside the VPC

  • A VPC attachment places one network interface in one subnet of each AZ you choose. Only instances in those AZs can use the transit gateway.
  • Use a small subnet of its own for this, a /28 in each AZ. Its route table and network ACL then apply only to traffic that arrives from the transit gateway.
  • The transit gateway keeps traffic in the AZ where it entered. For a stateful firewall in an inspection VPC this is a problem: the request and the reply can enter from different AZs and meet different firewall instances.
  • Appliance mode on the inspection VPC's attachment fixes it. The transit gateway then picks one AZ for a flow and keeps both directions there.
  • The VPC's own route tables still need routes that point to the transit gateway. The attachment adds none.

Route priority, peering and Connect

  • The longest prefix wins, as in a VPC. For the same prefix: static, then propagated from a VPC, then from a Direct Connect gateway, then Connect, then VPN.
  • A peering attachment joins two transit gateways, in the same Region or across Regions. It carries no propagation. You write static routes on both sides.
  • Traffic between Regions over a peering attachment stays on the AWS backbone and is encrypted.
  • A Connect attachment runs GRE tunnels and BGP to a virtual appliance, on top of a VPC or Direct Connect attachment. It gives an SD-WAN device dynamic routing and up to 5 Gbps per tunnel.
  • The MTU is 8,500 bytes between VPC, Direct Connect, Connect and peering attachments, and 1,500 on a VPN.

Direct Connect: connections and virtual interfaces

  • A connection is a physical port at a Direct Connect location. Dedicated: you own the port, at 1, 10, 100 or 400 Gbps. Hosted: a partner gives you a slice of theirs, from 50 Mbps to 25 Gbps.
  • A virtual interface (VIF) is one VLAN on the connection with one BGP session. Three types:
  • Private VIF: to the private addresses of VPCs, through a virtual private gateway or a Direct Connect gateway.
  • Transit VIF: to transit gateways, through a Direct Connect gateway.
  • Public VIF: to the public endpoints of AWS services in all Regions. AWS advertises its public ranges to you. It does not reach your VPCs.
  • Getting a port takes weeks. AWS gives you a letter of authorisation, and the facility or your carrier makes the cross-connect.
  • Traffic is not encrypted. Use MACsec on a dedicated 10 Gbps or faster port, or run an IPsec VPN over the connection.

Direct Connect gateway and LAG

  • A Direct Connect gateway is a global object. One VIF to it reaches VPCs or transit gateways in any Region. It costs nothing.
  • It takes either virtual private gateways or transit gateways, not both on the same gateway.
  • For each association you set allowed prefixes. With a transit gateway, these are exactly what AWS advertises to your router. Summarise: 10.0.0.0/8, not one entry per VPC.
  • It carries traffic between on-premises and AWS. It does not route from one VPC to another, or from one transit gateway to another.
  • A link aggregation group (LAG) bundles connections of the same speed at the same location into one logical link with LACP. It adds bandwidth. It does not add resilience, because all its links end on the same AWS device.

BGP on Direct Connect

  • Your router may advertise at most 100 prefixes on a private or transit VIF. With one more, the BGP session goes down and stays down until you advertise fewer.
  • BGP alone notices a dead link after the hold timer, 90 seconds by default. BFD notices in under a second. AWS has it ready on every VIF; it starts when you configure it on your router.
  • For the direction from AWS to you, the longest prefix decides first. Then local preference communities that you attach to your routes: 7224:7300 high, 7224:7200 medium, 7224:7100 low. Then the AS path length.
  • For the direction from you to AWS, your router decides. Set local preference there.
  • Resilience comes from separate locations. Two connections at each of two locations is the highest level; one at each of two locations is the usual production minimum.

Site-to-Site VPN with BGP

  • One VPN connection has two tunnels, to endpoints in two AZs. AWS replaces tunnel endpoints during maintenance, one at a time. Configure both tunnels, or maintenance is an outage.
  • Each tunnel carries up to 1.25 Gbps. On a transit gateway with BGP, ECMP spreads flows over several tunnels and several connections. On a virtual private gateway there is no ECMP.
  • With BGP, routes appear and disappear with the tunnel. With static routing, you list the prefixes by hand.
  • On a virtual private gateway your router may advertise 100 routes; on a transit gateway, 1,000.
  • Dead peer detection waits 30 seconds by default before it declares the tunnel down.
  • IPsec adds headers. Lower the TCP maximum segment size on your router, or large packets are dropped inside the tunnel.
  • An accelerated VPN enters AWS at the nearest edge location. It works only on a transit gateway.

Route 53 Resolver endpoints

  • Every VPC has a resolver at the base of its range plus two. It answers private hosted zones, VPC names and public names. It is reachable only from inside the VPC.
  • An inbound endpoint gives that resolver IP addresses in your subnets. The data centre's DNS servers forward your AWS zones to those addresses.
  • An outbound endpoint with a forwarding rule does the reverse: queries for corp.example.com leave through it to the DNS servers on-premises.
  • A rule works only in the VPCs it is associated with. Share the rule with AWS RAM and associate it with every VPC. The queries of all those VPCs use the one outbound endpoint; no DNS traffic crosses the transit gateway until the endpoint forwards it.
  • The most specific domain name in the rules wins. A system rule for a subdomain keeps it local while its parent is forwarded.
  • Do not forward a zone out that the other side forwards back in. The query circles until it times out.

Shared VPC with RAM

  • The network account owns a VPC and shares subnets with other accounts of the same organisation through AWS Resource Access Manager.
  • A participant account launches instances, databases and load balancers into the shared subnet. It owns and pays for them. It cannot change the subnet, the route tables, the network ACLs or the gateways.
  • The owner cannot see into or delete the participants' resources.
  • Security groups belong to the account that created them. Participants can reference each other's groups by ID.
  • What you gain: one network instead of fifty, no transit gateway charge between the teams, and central control of routing.
  • What you give up: the VPC is one blast radius. All participants share its address space and its quotas.
  • An AZ name such as ap-south-1a maps to a different physical zone in each account. Compare AZ IDs, such as aps1-az1.

PrivateLink

  • The provider puts a Network Load Balancer in front of the service and creates an endpoint service. The consumer creates an interface endpoint: a network interface with an address from the consumer's own subnet (architecture Day 24).
  • No route connects the two VPCs. So overlapping address ranges do not matter, and the consumer can reach that one service and nothing else.
  • Connections go one way: from consumer to provider.
  • The provider sees the load balancer's address as the source, not the client's. Turn on Proxy Protocol v2 to receive the client address and the endpoint ID.
  • The provider approves each consumer, by a list of allowed principals and optionally by accepting each request.
  • An endpoint can be created only in AZs where the provider's load balancer is present. Match by AZ ID.
  • An interface endpoint can be reached from on-premises and from other VPCs, as long as they can route to its address.

VPC Lattice

  • Lattice connects services, not networks. It works at the application layer: HTTP, HTTPS, gRPC and TLS.
  • A service has listeners, rules and target groups: instances, IP addresses, Lambda functions, load balancers, ECS tasks and Kubernetes pods.
  • A service network is a group of services. You associate services with it and VPCs with it. A client in an associated VPC can call every service in the network.
  • Clients reach a service by its DNS name, which resolves to link-local addresses. No route tables change, and overlapping ranges do not matter.
  • An auth policy on the service network or the service is an IAM policy for requests: which principal may call which path. The caller signs requests with SigV4.
  • You share service networks and services across accounts with RAM.

Cloud WAN

  • Cloud WAN is a managed global network. You write one core network policy, a JSON document. AWS builds a router in each Region you list, and connects them with dynamic routing.
  • A segment is a routing domain that spans all Regions. It does the work of a set of transit gateway route tables.
  • Attachments (VPC, VPN, Connect, Direct Connect gateway, an existing transit gateway route table) join a segment by their tags. The policy can require approval.
  • Segments are isolated from each other unless the policy shares routes between them.
  • Every policy change is a new version with a change set that you review and then apply.
  • Choose it over transit gateways with peering when you have several Regions and do not want to maintain static routes between them.

A security review finds that dev can open connections to prod. Nobody asked for it. Both attachments use the default route table, or a default route sends everything through a hub that knows every VPC. aws ec2 search-transit-gateway-routes on the table that dev is associated with. Look for a route that covers prod's range, including 0.0.0.0/0. A route table per environment, with no propagation between them. Blackhole routes for the other environments' ranges.
Through the inspection VPC, some connections work and others hang. It depends on which AZ the two ends are in. Appliance mode is off. The request and the reply enter the inspection VPC in different AZs and meet different firewall instances. The firewall logs show flows with only one direction. The pairs that fail are always in different AZs. Turn on appliance mode on the inspection VPC's attachment.
Instances in one AZ cannot use the transit gateway. The other AZs are fine. The VPC attachment has no subnet in that AZ. aws ec2 describe-transit-gateway-vpc-attachments and read SubnetIds. The Direct Connect port is up and the BGP session is down. It started when a new site was added on-premises. The router now advertises more than 100 prefixes on the VIF. The VIF shows BGP status down. The CloudWatch metric VirtualInterfaceBgpStatus is 0. On the router, the session is idle. Advertise a summary, or a default route. Alarm on the prefix count before it reaches 100.
Direct Connect fails. For about 90 seconds nothing works. Then everything is slow. Without BFD, BGP waits for the hold timer. After failover, a 10 Gbps load runs on 1.25 Gbps tunnels. The time of the outage against the BGP logs. TunnelDataOut on the VPN flat at its ceiling. Configure BFD on the router. Size the backup: several VPN connections with ECMP, or a second Direct Connect at another location.
Small requests work over the VPN. Large responses and file transfers hang. Packets are larger than the tunnel can carry. The transit gateway does not send the ICMP message that tells the sender to use smaller packets, so path MTU discovery fails. ping with the don't-fragment flag and growing sizes: it stops answering above a certain size. Clamp the TCP MSS on the customer gateway. Set the MTU of instances that talk across the VPN to 1,500 or less.
A new VPC cannot resolve on-premises names. Older VPCs can. The forwarding rule is not associated with the new VPC. Or the endpoint's security group does not allow port 53 on both UDP and TCP. aws route53resolver list-resolver-rule-associations. Resolver query logs for the VPC. Associate the shared rule when the account is created, as part of account vending. Route 53 Profiles can do it for a whole set of rules and zones.
Consumers of a PrivateLink service in one AZ get timeouts. Consumers in other AZs are fine. The provider's load balancer has no healthy target in that AZ, and cross-zone load balancing is off. On the provider side: the target group's healthy host count per AZ. Targets in every AZ the service is offered in, or cross-zone load balancing on.
Traffic to one on-premises subnet takes the VPN while Direct Connect is healthy. The router advertises that subnet as its own, longer prefix on the VPN. The longest prefix wins before the type of link is compared. Search the transit gateway route table for that address: the matching route points to the VPN attachment. Advertise the same prefixes on both paths.

Hands-on lab

Goal: build the three-VPC hub from the diagram, give the transit gateway three route tables, read them, make the one-way mistake from the simulator and close it with a blackhole route. About 40 minutes.

Cost: about $0.15 for an hour: three attachments at $0.05 an hour each. No instances and no data. Attachments are billed for as long as they exist, so run the cleanup step.

  1. Choose a Region and create three VPCs, each with one small subnet for the attachment. 
    export AWS_DEFAULT_REGION=ap-south-1

make_vpc() {   # $1 = name, $2 = second octet of the range. Prints "vpc-id subnet-id".
  local vpc=$(aws ec2 create-vpc --cidr-block 10.$2.0.0/16 \
    --tag-specifications "ResourceType=vpc,Tags=[{Key=Name,Value=mastery-day03-$1}]" \
    --query Vpc.VpcId --output text)
  local subnet=$(aws ec2 create-subnet --vpc-id $vpc --cidr-block 10.$2.255.0/28 \
    --query Subnet.SubnetId --output text)
  echo "$vpc $subnet"
}
read VPC_DEV SUB_DEV       <<< "$(make_vpc dev 2)"
read VPC_PROD SUB_PROD     <<< "$(make_vpc prod 1)"
read VPC_SHARED SUB_SHARED <<< "$(make_vpc shared 0)"
  2. Create the transit gateway with both defaults turned off. Without this, every attachment would join one default route table. Repeat the second command until it prints available; it takes a minute or two. 
    TGW=$(aws ec2 create-transit-gateway --description mastery-day03 \
  --options DefaultRouteTableAssociation=disable,DefaultRouteTablePropagation=disable \
  --query TransitGateway.TransitGatewayId --output text)

aws ec2 describe-transit-gateways --transit-gateway-ids $TGW \
  --query 'TransitGateways[0].State' --output text
  3. Attach the three VPCs. Repeat the last command until all three are available. 
    attach() {   # $1 = VPC, $2 = subnet
  aws ec2 create-transit-gateway-vpc-attachment --transit-gateway-id $TGW \
    --vpc-id $1 --subnet-ids $2 \
    --query TransitGatewayVpcAttachment.TransitGatewayAttachmentId --output text
}
ATT_DEV=$(attach $VPC_DEV $SUB_DEV)
ATT_PROD=$(attach $VPC_PROD $SUB_PROD)
ATT_SHARED=$(attach $VPC_SHARED $SUB_SHARED)

aws ec2 describe-transit-gateway-vpc-attachments \
  --filters Name=transit-gateway-id,Values=$TGW \
  --query 'TransitGatewayVpcAttachments[].[TransitGatewayAttachmentId,State]' --output text
  4. Create three route tables. Then set the associations (which table each attachment reads) and the propagations (which tables learn each attachment's range). Wait half a minute after creating the tables before you associate. 
    make_rt() {   # $1 = name
  aws ec2 create-transit-gateway-route-table --transit-gateway-id $TGW \
    --tag-specifications "ResourceType=transit-gateway-route-table,Tags=[{Key=Name,Value=$1}]" \
    --query TransitGatewayRouteTable.TransitGatewayRouteTableId --output text
}
RT_DEV=$(make_rt rt-dev); RT_PROD=$(make_rt rt-prod); RT_HUB=$(make_rt rt-hub)

assoc() { aws ec2 associate-transit-gateway-route-table \
  --transit-gateway-route-table-id $1 --transit-gateway-attachment-id $2; }
prop()  { aws ec2 enable-transit-gateway-route-table-propagation \
  --transit-gateway-route-table-id $1 --transit-gateway-attachment-id $2; }

assoc $RT_DEV $ATT_DEV;  assoc $RT_PROD $ATT_PROD;  assoc $RT_HUB $ATT_SHARED
prop $RT_DEV $ATT_SHARED;  prop $RT_PROD $ATT_SHARED
prop $RT_HUB $ATT_DEV;  prop $RT_HUB $ATT_PROD;  prop $RT_HUB $ATT_SHARED
  5. Read the three tables. Before each command, say which ranges you expect. 
    routes() {   # $1 = route table
  aws ec2 search-transit-gateway-routes --transit-gateway-route-table-id $1 \
    --filters Name=state,Values=active,blackhole \
    --query 'Routes[].[DestinationCidrBlock,Type,State]' --output text
}
routes $RT_DEV; echo; routes $RT_PROD; echo; routes $RT_HUB
    Expect rt-dev and rt-prod to hold only 10.0.0.0/16, and rt-hub to hold all three ranges. Dev has no route to 10.1.0.0/16. That missing line is the whole isolation.
  6. Make the one-way mistake: let dev propagate into prod's table. Then read both tables again. 
    prop $RT_PROD $ATT_DEV
routes $RT_PROD; echo; routes $RT_DEV
    rt-prod now has 10.2.0.0/16. rt-dev has not changed. A packet from prod would reach dev, and the reply would be dropped.
  7. Close it with a blackhole route, and read the table. A static route beats a propagated route for the same prefix, so the state of 10.2.0.0/16 is now blackhole. Then remove the mistaken propagation as well. 
    aws ec2 create-transit-gateway-route --transit-gateway-route-table-id $RT_PROD \
  --destination-cidr-block 10.2.0.0/16 --blackhole
routes $RT_PROD

aws ec2 disable-transit-gateway-route-table-propagation \
  --transit-gateway-route-table-id $RT_PROD --transit-gateway-attachment-id $ATT_DEV
    No packet has moved in this lab. For real traffic, each VPC's own route table also needs a route to the gateway: aws ec2 create-route --route-table-id … --destination-cidr-block 10.0.0.0/8 --transit-gateway-id $TGW.
  8. Clean up. The attachments first; they take a few minutes. Repeat the describe command until every state is deleted, then run the rest.

Interview questions

How do you stop the dev VPC from reaching prod when both attach to the same transit gateway?

With route tables on the transit gateway. I turn off default association and default propagation. Dev's attachment is associated with rt-dev and prod's with rt-prod. Shared services and the hybrid links propagate into both tables; dev and prod propagate only into the hub table. Dev's table then has no route for prod's range, and prod's has none for dev's, so the packet is dropped in both directions. If either table has a default route for central egress, that route matches the other environment too, so I add blackhole routes for the other environment's range. I verify with search-transit-gateway-routes on both tables and with Reachability Analyzer.

What is the difference between association and propagation on a transit gateway?

Association is about traffic that arrives: an attachment is associated with exactly one route table, and packets that enter from it are routed by that table. Propagation is about routes: an attachment can propagate its prefixes into any number of route tables, so those tables learn how to reach it. They are independent. In a hub-and-spoke design a spoke is associated with its own table and propagates into the hub's table, not into its own.

You route all traffic between VPCs through a firewall in an inspection VPC. Some flows fail, depending on the AZ. Why?

The transit gateway keeps traffic in the AZ where it entered. The request enters the inspection VPC in the source's AZ and the reply enters in the destination's AZ. If these differ, the two directions pass different firewall instances, and a stateful firewall drops a flow of which it saw only half. The fix is appliance mode on the inspection VPC's attachment: the transit gateway then chooses one AZ per flow and uses it for both directions.

Explain private, public and transit virtual interfaces.

Each is one VLAN with one BGP session on a Direct Connect connection. A private VIF reaches the private addresses of VPCs, through a virtual private gateway or a Direct Connect gateway. A transit VIF reaches transit gateways through a Direct Connect gateway, and from there every VPC attached to them. A public VIF reaches the public endpoints of AWS services such as S3 in all Regions; AWS advertises its public ranges and I advertise public addresses that I own. A public VIF gives no access to a VPC.

Your Direct Connect fails over to VPN. What can go wrong?

Three things. Detection: without BFD the router waits for the BGP hold timer, about 90 seconds of lost traffic. Capacity: a VPN tunnel carries 1.25 Gbps, so a 10 Gbps load does not fit unless I have several VPN connections with ECMP on a transit gateway. Packet size: the VPN's MTU is lower, so flows that used large packets can hang unless the MSS is clamped. There is also the question of whether the backup works at all. I test it on a schedule by shutting the BGP session on purpose.

The BGP session on a transit VIF went down when the network team added a site. The port is up. What happened?

The router crossed the limit of 100 advertised prefixes on a private or transit VIF. AWS takes the session down when it receives more. The fix is to summarise the routes, or advertise a default route, and then the session comes back. To prevent it I alarm on the number of advertised prefixes well below 100, and I agree an address plan with the network team that can be summarised.

Servers on-premises must resolve names in your private hosted zones, and instances must resolve on-premises names. How?

Two Resolver endpoints in a central VPC. An inbound endpoint gives the VPC resolver addresses that are reachable over Direct Connect or VPN; the on-premises DNS servers forward the AWS zones to them. An outbound endpoint with a forwarding rule sends queries for the on-premises domain to the DNS servers there. I share the rule with RAM and associate it with every VPC, and I associate the private hosted zones with the central VPC. The endpoints' security groups allow port 53 over UDP and TCP. I check that no zone is forwarded in both directions, which would make a loop.

A partner's VPC uses the same CIDR as yours and you must call their API. What are the options?

Peering is refused and a transit gateway cannot route between two identical ranges. PrivateLink is the direct answer: the partner publishes the API as an endpoint service behind a Network Load Balancer, and I create an interface endpoint that takes an address in my own subnet. Addresses never have to be unique across the two sides, and the partner gets no route into my network. If the API is HTTP and both sides use it, VPC Lattice also works across overlapping ranges. A private NAT gateway with a small routable range on each side is the last resort.

When do you choose a shared VPC over one VPC per account with a transit gateway?

When many accounts belong together and talk to each other a lot: the teams of one product, for example. A shared VPC gives them account-level separation of IAM, billing and quotas, with one network that a central team runs. There is no transit gateway charge between them and no route tables to segment. I keep separate VPCs where I need a network boundary: between prod and non-prod, between business units, or for a workload with its own compliance scope. Those separate VPCs then meet at a transit gateway.

What does Cloud WAN give you that transit gateways with peering do not?

Peering between transit gateways carries no propagation, so every Region pair needs static routes on both sides, and every new range means editing them. Cloud WAN routes dynamically between its Regions. Segmentation is declared once in a policy and holds in all Regions, and attachments join a segment by tag instead of by hand. Changes are versioned and reviewed as change sets. The price is a fixed hourly charge per Region, so with one or two Regions and a stable set of ranges, transit gateways with peering are still simpler and cheaper.
