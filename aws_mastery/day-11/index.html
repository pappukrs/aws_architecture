Messaging and orchestration

A queue between two services buys you three things: the producer never waits for the consumer, a burst becomes a backlog instead of an outage, and a consumer that dies loses nothing. It costs you one thing, and most teams discover it in production: the message that was processed twice. SQS promises at-least-once delivery, and so does every other system on this page once you read the small print; the consumer that charges a card and then crashes before deleting the message will charge it again. This page is about building on that promise instead of fighting it. First the queue: SQS standard against FIFO, the visibility timeout, long polling, dead-letter queues and redrive. Then the fan-out and routing layer: SNS with filter policies, EventBridge with buses, rules, Pipes and the Scheduler. Then the orchestrators: Step Functions standard against express, retries, and the saga pattern for a process that must undo itself. Then the streams and the rest: Kinesis against MSK, Amazon MQ for protocols you did not choose, and AppSync for clients that want to be told. The centre of the page is the interview question: a message is processed twice. Is that a bug? How do you make the consumer safe?

The diagram

The whole picture. Producers on the left send to a queue, publish to a topic, put events on a bus or put records on a stream, and every one of those calls is retried on a 5xx, which is the first place a duplicate is born. SQS in the middle is a buffer with a lease on every message: a consumer receives a message, it becomes invisible for the visibility timeout, and if the consumer deletes it in time it is gone, otherwise it comes back. The standard queue scales without limit and promises only that nothing is lost; the FIFO queue orders messages inside a group and removes producer duplicates for five minutes. A message received too many times goes to the dead-letter queue, from which a redrive sends it back. Consumers on the left are where correctness lives: claim, act, delete, in that order. Above the queue, SNS copies one publish to every subscription that its filter policy allows, and EventBridge matches events against rules and sends them to targets, with Pipes for point-to-point plumbing and the Scheduler for time. Step Functions holds a multi-step process as data, retries each step and runs the compensation when a step fails. On the right, Kinesis and MSK are logs rather than queues: readers keep their own position and nothing is deleted by reading; AppSync pushes data to clients and Amazon MQ runs the brokers for protocols you inherited. The strip underneath says what each one promises, and the one thing none of them does. Bottom left are the metrics, bottom right is the answer to the question of the day.

Processed twice: where the duplicates come from, and what they cost

Thursday, 11:00. Finance asks why a customer was charged twice. The queue did what it promised; the question is how many duplicate deliveries your setup produces in an hour, where they come from, and whether the consumer turns them into duplicate side effects. Model: four sources of a second delivery. The producer retries a SendMessage whose response was lost, about one call in 20,000 on a normal day, and each retry is a new message with a new id on a standard queue; a FIFO queue drops it when the producer sends a deduplication id. The queue itself, on standard, re-delivers a message on rare internal retries, taken as one in 100,000. The visibility timeout expires before the work is done: if the work takes longer than the timeout every message is redelivered, and all of its retries fail the same way until maxReceiveCount sends it, already processed, to the DLQ; if the work's slow tail (taken as 3× the typical time) crosses the timeout, 2% of messages are redelivered. The consumer fails after the side effect and before the delete at the rate you pick, and that message comes back after the timeout. A consumer with no idempotency turns every duplicate into a duplicate side effect; a claim with a conditional write catches all of them except the ones whose claim and act are not atomic, taken as 2% of the consumer-failure duplicates when the claim is written after the act; an idempotent operation catches all of them. FIFO exactly-once processing removes the producer and queue duplicates; it does nothing about the visibility and consumer ones.

How it works inside

SQS standard: a lease, not a lock

  • A queue is stored redundantly across AZs. SendMessage writes a copy to several servers and acknowledges; ReceiveMessage samples a subset of those servers, which is why a receive on a nearly empty queue can return nothing while a message exists (the docs call it short polling's sampling) and why order is best-effort: a message that landed on a server that was not sampled comes later.
  • Receiving does not remove. The message becomes invisible for the visibility timeout (30 s by default, up to 12 hours), and the consumer must call DeleteMessage with the receipt handle before the timeout, or the message reappears for another consumer. ChangeMessageVisibility extends the lease for long work; a Lambda event source mapping does this for you up to the function's timeout.
  • Throughput is effectively unlimited per queue, with no provisioning. In flight at once: 120,000 messages (received, not deleted); beyond that receives return nothing. Message size 256 KB by default, raisable to 1 MiB; bigger payloads go to S3 with a pointer (the extended client library does it).
  • Long polling (WaitTimeSeconds up to 20) makes a receive wait until a message arrives and queries every server, so it returns fewer false empties and costs a twentieth as much when the queue is idle. There is no reason to short-poll a production queue.

SQS FIFO: order and dedup, per group

  • Every message carries a MessageGroupId. Messages in one group are delivered in order and, crucially, one at a time: while a message from group A is in flight, no other message of group A is delivered to anyone. Groups are the unit of parallelism; a queue with one group is single-threaded.
  • MessageDeduplicationId (or a SHA-256 of the body with content-based deduplication) makes a second send with the same id within 5 minutes a no-op. This is producer-side dedup: it removes a retried send, not a redelivered message.
  • Throughput: 300 messages a second per API action without batching, 3,000 with batches of 10; high throughput mode partitions by group and reaches tens of thousands a second, 70,000 with batching in the largest Regions. 20,000 in flight.
  • "Exactly-once processing" means a message is delivered once and stays unavailable until the visibility timeout passes; if your consumer fails to delete it in time it comes back, in order, and blocks its group. A poison message in a FIFO group stops the group until the DLQ takes it.

Dead-letter queues and redrive

  • A redrive policy on the source queue names a DLQ and a maxReceiveCount (1–1,000). Each receive increments ApproximateReceiveCount; past the limit, SQS moves the message to the DLQ instead of making it visible again. The DLQ must be the same type (standard or FIFO) and in the same account and Region.
  • The DLQ's retention clock is the original send time, not the move time: a 4-day retention with a message that spent 3 days in the source leaves one day to look at it. Give DLQs the full 14 days.
  • A redrive (StartMessageMoveTask) sends DLQ messages back to the source, or to any queue, at a rate you choose. It is for after the fix. Redriving into an unfixed consumer is a loop with extra steps.
  • A Lambda consumer without ReportBatchItemFailures makes the whole batch fail when one message fails, so nine good messages are retried with the bad one and arrive in the DLQ together. Turn partial batch responses on, always.

SNS: one publish, many copies

  • A topic takes a publish and delivers a copy to each subscription: SQS queues, Lambda functions, HTTP endpoints, email, SMS, mobile push, Firehose. Delivery to SQS and Lambda is free and reliable; HTTP endpoints get a retry policy of your choosing (up to about an hour by default, 23 days configurable) and then a DLQ.
  • A filter policy per subscription selects messages by attributes or, since 2022, by fields of the JSON body: exact match, prefix, numeric ranges, anything-but, exists. The topic does the routing so the subscriber receives only what it asked for and pays nothing for the rest.
  • Raw message delivery hands the subscriber the payload as sent instead of the SNS envelope; without it, a queue consumer parses JSON inside JSON. FIFO topics deliver in order to FIFO queues only, with the same group and dedup semantics as the queue.
  • No retention: a subscriber that is unreachable gets retries and then nothing. The reliable pattern is SNS → SQS → consumer: the queue holds the message for the consumer that is down; the topic only copies it.

EventBridge: a bus with rules

  • An event bus receives events as JSON with a source, detail-type and detail. The default bus carries events from AWS services (an EC2 state change, a CodePipeline stage, a GuardDuty finding); custom buses carry yours; partner buses carry SaaS events. Rules match on any field with patterns (equals, prefix, numeric, exists, anything-but, wildcards) and send to up to five targets each, with input transformation.
  • Delivery is at-least-once with a retry policy (up to 24 hours and 185 attempts by default) and a DLQ per target. Latency is typically about half a second; it is not a queue for a tight loop. Throughput defaults to thousands of PutEvents a second in the big Regions, raisable.
  • Archive and replay: an archive records events matching a pattern for a retention you set, and a replay sends them back through the rules for a time window, to the rules you name. That is the recovery tool after a bad deploy of a consumer.
  • Pipes connect one source (SQS, Kinesis, DynamoDB Streams, MSK, MQ) to one target with optional filtering and an enrichment step (Lambda, Step Functions, API), replacing the glue function. The Scheduler runs one-off and recurring schedules (cron, rate, at) with a flexible window and time zone, invoking over 270 services directly, at a million schedules an account.

Step Functions: Standard and Express

  • A state machine is a JSON definition (Amazon States Language) of states: Task (call a service or a function), Choice, Parallel, Map, Wait, Pass, Succeed, Fail. The state's output is the next state's input; the data flowing through is the execution's memory, up to 256 KB. Over 220 service integrations call APIs directly, without a Lambda in between.
  • Standard: an execution runs up to one year, every state transition is recorded durably, the execution is exactly-once (a second StartExecution with the same name within 90 days is rejected), and you pay $0.025 per 1,000 transitions. For long-lived, human-in-the-loop, auditable processes.
  • Express: up to five minutes, at-least-once (asynchronous) or at-most-once (synchronous StartSyncExecution), history to CloudWatch Logs, priced per request and duration, $1 per million plus about $0.00001667 per GB-second. For high-volume, short, idempotent work: event processing, API orchestration, the body of a Map.
  • Integration patterns: request-response (call and continue), .sync (call and wait for the job: a Batch job, a Glue run, an ECS task, a child workflow), and .waitForTaskToken (pause until something calls SendTaskSuccess with the token: a human approval, an external system). Distributed Map runs up to 10,000 child executions over an S3 listing or a JSON array.

Retries, catches and the saga

  • A Task state's Retry lists error names (States.TaskFailed, States.Timeout, Lambda.ServiceException, your own), an interval, a backoff rate, a maximum of attempts and, since 2023, jitter and a cap. Retry transient errors; do not retry a validation error 20 times.
  • A Catch routes an error to another state with the error in the input. That is how a workflow fails gracefully: catch, record, compensate, then end in a Fail state with a cause someone can read.
  • The saga pattern: a sequence of steps, each with a compensating step that undoes it (reserve stock / release stock, charge card / refund, create shipment / cancel shipment). When step N fails, the Catch runs the compensations for steps N−1 down to 1. Each step and each compensation must be idempotent, because the workflow may run them again after a retry.
  • A task running in Lambda has its own timeout and Lambda's own retries for asynchronous invocations; set TimeoutSeconds on the Task state and HeartbeatSeconds on long callbacks so a hung step becomes a States.Timeout instead of a year-long wait.

Kinesis Data Streams

  • A stream is a set of shards; a record goes to the shard chosen by the hash of its partition key and gets a sequence number. Each shard accepts 1 MB/s or 1,000 records a second and serves 2 MB/s to all consumers together, or 2 MB/s per consumer with enhanced fan-out (push over HTTP/2, up to 20 consumers). Records up to 1 MB; retention 24 hours by default, up to 365 days.
  • Reading is by iterator: a consumer asks for records from a position (trim horizon, latest, a sequence number, a timestamp) and keeps its own checkpoint (the KCL stores it in DynamoDB; a Lambda event source mapping keeps it for you). Reading deletes nothing; two consumers see the same records.
  • On-demand mode scales shards for you and bills per GB; provisioned bills per shard-hour and needs resharding (split and merge) as traffic changes. A hot partition key is a hot shard: WriteProvisionedThroughputExceeded on one shard while the others idle.
  • Order is per shard. A Lambda consumer runs one invocation per shard at a time (or up to 10 with parallelization factor, ordered per key), and a failing batch blocks the shard until it succeeds, the record expires, or the retry and bisect settings give up: a poison record stalls the shard, and IteratorAge is the alarm.

MSK and Amazon MQ

  • MSK runs Apache Kafka: brokers across AZs, topics split into partitions, each a replicated ordered log; producers choose a partition by key; consumer groups share partitions among members and commit offsets. Retention is by time or size per topic, as long as you pay for disk. Provisioned clusters (you pick brokers and storage; Express brokers simplify it) or MSK Serverless (per partition-hour and GB). Connect for sinks and sources, Schema Registry via Glue.
  • Kafka is for when you have the ecosystem (Flink, Debezium, Connect, a team that speaks it), need retention of weeks to forever, topics with hundreds of partitions, or hundreds of MB/s. For most AWS-native work Kinesis is the same shape with nothing to operate; the choice is usually about the team, not the technology.
  • Amazon MQ is a managed ActiveMQ or RabbitMQ broker in your VPC: AMQP, JMS/OpenWire, MQTT, STOMP, the protocols an existing application already speaks. Active/standby across AZs for ActiveMQ (failover is a reconnect, tens of seconds), a 3-node cluster for RabbitMQ. You size the broker and it has a ceiling.
  • MQ is the migration answer, not the greenfield one: it moves a JMS application to AWS without a rewrite. A new system that wants a queue wants SQS; one that wants a broker's routing wants EventBridge or SNS.

AppSync: the server calls the client

  • A managed GraphQL API: a schema, resolvers (JavaScript or VTL, single or pipeline) that map fields to data sources (DynamoDB, Lambda, RDS via the Data API, OpenSearch, HTTP, EventBridge), authorisation by API key, IAM, Cognito, OIDC or a Lambda authoriser, per field. Caching per resolver.
  • Subscriptions: a client subscribes over WebSocket to a mutation; when the mutation runs, AppSync pushes the result to every subscribed client, filtered server-side. That is how a dashboard, a chat or an order tracker gets live data without polling and without you running a WebSocket fleet. The Events API does the same for plain pub/sub channels without a GraphQL schema.
  • Limits to know: 256 KB per request and response, a 30-second resolver timeout, and subscriptions deliver what the mutation returned, so the mutation's selection set must include what the subscriber needs.
  • Pricing is per operation ($4 per million queries and mutations), per real-time update and per connection-minute. For a server-to-server API with no live clients, API Gateway and Lambda are cheaper and simpler.

Consumers: Lambda or a worker fleet

  • A Lambda event source mapping polls the queue for you, invokes the function with a batch (up to 10 messages for FIFO; up to 10,000 with a batching window of up to 5 minutes for standard), deletes the messages the function reported as processed, and scales from 5 concurrent pollers by up to 300 a minute, capped by maximum concurrency on the mapping (2–1,000) and the account's concurrency. Set the queue's visibility timeout to six times the function timeout, or a slow function's batch is redelivered while it is still running.
  • A worker on ECS or EC2 runs a loop: long-poll, process, delete, with a thread pool. It is the answer when the work is long (more than 15 minutes), needs a warm connection pool or a large model in memory, or when the consumer is a legacy process. Scale the service on ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible divided by the task count (backlog per task), not on CPU, which is idle while waiting.
  • The order of operations is the whole story: receive, claim the business key, act, delete. Delete first and a crash loses the message; act before claiming and a crash duplicates the act; claim after acting and a crash in between does both.
  • Backpressure is free: a consumer that is slow leaves messages in the queue, and the age metric tells you. A consumer that must not fall behind needs more concurrency or a faster handler, never a bigger queue.

Idempotency: the consumer's promise

  • An operation is idempotent when running it twice has the effect of running it once. Naturally idempotent: set a value, upsert a row by key, delete by key, put an object. Not: increment, append, insert without a unique key, send an email, charge a card.
  • For the second kind, an idempotency key turns the operation into the first kind: a business id (order 4711's payment) that is stored with the result the first time, so a second run with the same key finds the record and returns the stored result. A conditional write (DynamoDB attribute_not_exists, a unique index) makes the claim atomic across concurrent duplicates; a TTL on the record bounds the table.
  • Pass the key downstream: payment providers, email APIs and your own services accept an Idempotency-Key header for exactly this reason. Lambda Powertools has an idempotency decorator that does the DynamoDB dance for you, including the in-progress state and expiry.
  • Choose the key from the business, not from the transport: the SQS message id changes when the producer retries, so a dedup on it misses the most common duplicate.

Which one, in a sentence

  • SQS: work for one of N identical workers, with a buffer. SNS: tell everyone who asked, now, cheaply, at any scale. EventBridge: route events by content across teams and accounts, with AWS and SaaS events included, and replay. Step Functions: a process with steps, retries, branches, waits and a history someone will read. Scheduler: at a time or on a schedule.
  • Kinesis: an ordered log of records many readers consume at their own pace, AWS-native. MSK: the same when you have Kafka. Amazon MQ: a broker for an application that already speaks AMQP or JMS. AppSync: push data to browsers and phones.
  • The patterns that combine them: SNS → SQS (fan-out with buffering per consumer), EventBridge → SQS → Lambda (routing with a buffer and a DLQ), SQS → Pipe → Step Functions (a queue that starts a workflow per message), Kinesis → Lambda (ordered processing per key), DynamoDB Streams → Pipe → EventBridge (changes as events).
  • What they all share: at-least-once delivery, per-key order at best, a retry policy that ends in a DLQ, and the need for a consumer that does not mind seeing a message twice.
