Day 22 of 30 · Week 4 — Enterprise Architecture

Connecting VPCs

A real company does not have one VPC. It has one for production, one for development, one for shared tools, often dozens. Week 4 is about that scale. Today you connect VPCs to each other privately: first with peering, which is simple but stops working as the number grows, then with a Transit Gateway that acts as a central hub.

The diagram

Top: a peering connection joins exactly two VPCs, and each side needs a route to the other. A is peered with B and B with C, yet A cannot reach C: traffic never passes through a VPC to a third one. Bottom: every VPC attaches once to a Transit Gateway. Each VPC has a single route pointing at the hub, and the hub's own route table sends traffic to the right VPC.

Concepts

VPC peering

A private link between two VPCs. Instances talk using private IPs, as if on one network.

  • Works across accounts and across Regions.
  • Traffic stays on the AWS network, never the public internet.
  • No hourly charge and no bandwidth limit; you pay only for data transfer.
  • One side requests, the other accepts, then both add a route.

The three peering rules

  • No overlapping CIDRs. Two VPCs that both use 10.0.0.0/16 cannot be peered. This is why Day 2 said to plan ranges early.
  • Not transitive. A–B and B–C do not give A–C.
  • No sharing of gateways. A cannot use B's Internet Gateway, NAT Gateway or VPN through the peering.

And as always, security groups must still allow the traffic.

Transit Gateway

A managed router that sits in the middle. Everything attaches to it once.

  • Attachments: VPCs, VPN connections, Direct Connect, and other Transit Gateways in other Regions.
  • It is transitive: anything attached can reach anything else, subject to its route tables.
  • Each VPC attachment uses one subnet in every AZ you want covered.
  • Billed per attachment per hour and per GB, so it costs more than peering.

Transit Gateway route tables

The hub has its own route tables, and you can have several. That is how you keep networks apart while sharing one hub.

  • Production and development VPCs can both reach a shared-services VPC, but not each other.
  • All traffic leaving for the internet can be forced through one inspection VPC.
  • In each VPC, one summary route such as 10.0.0.0/8 → tgw covers every other network.

Check yourself

Why does peering not scale?

It is not transitive, so every pair of VPCs that must talk needs its own connection: n × (n − 1) ÷ 2 for a full mesh. Ten VPCs need 45 connections and nine routes in every route table. A hub needs ten attachments and one route.

The peering connection is active, but an instance in A cannot ping one in B. What do you check?

Routes and security groups. Both VPCs need a route to the other's CIDR pointing at the peering connection, in the route tables of the subnets involved. Then the security group on the target must allow the traffic from the other VPC's range.

Two teams each built a VPC with 10.0.0.0/16. Can they be connected?

Not by peering or a Transit Gateway; overlapping ranges cannot be routed between. One must be re-addressed, or they expose specific services to each other with PrivateLink (Day 24), which does not need unique ranges.

VPC A has no NAT Gateway but is peered with B, which has one. Can instances in A reach the internet through B?

No. Peering only carries traffic whose source and destination are in the two VPCs. It cannot be used to reach another VPC's Internet Gateway, NAT Gateway or VPN.

Does traffic over a peering connection cross the public internet?

No. It travels on the AWS network between private IP addresses, even between Regions.

All VPCs share one Transit Gateway. How do you stop development from reaching production?

Use separate Transit Gateway route tables. Associate the development attachments with a table that has routes only to shared services, and likewise for production, so neither table contains the other's range.

Two VPCs exchange a very large volume of data. Peering or Transit Gateway?

Peering. It has no per-GB processing charge and no bandwidth cap, so for a heavy, fixed pair it is cheaper and faster. You can use both: a Transit Gateway for general connectivity and a peering for that one pair.

Why have several VPCs at all, instead of one large one?

Isolation. Separate VPCs, usually in separate accounts, keep a mistake or a breach in development away from production, let teams work independently, and make costs and permissions clear. Day 29 builds that account structure.
