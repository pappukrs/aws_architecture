      6. Explain it out loud

      Answer each one aloud in a few sentences before opening it.

      Why are some arrows solid and some dashed?

      A solid arrow carries the customer's request, and the customer waits for it. A dashed arrow is not on that path: the DNS lookup that comes before the request, copies made in the background, work taken from the queue later, and the route that is used only after a failover.

      Why is the load balancer in public subnets while the tasks are in private ones?

      Only the load balancer has to be reachable from outside, so only it gets a route from the Internet Gateway. The tasks have no public address and accept port 8080 from alb-sg alone. To reach the internet themselves they go out through a NAT Gateway, which does not let connections in.

      Why is there a queue between the app and the worker, instead of a direct call?

      The customer gets an answer as soon as the order is saved. If the worker or the mail service is down, orders are still accepted and the messages wait. A sudden spike fills the queue instead of overloading the worker. Failed messages are retried, and after three tries set aside in the dead-letter queue.

      The shop has no visitors at 3 a.m. What still costs money?

      Everything billed by the hour: two NAT Gateways, the load balancer, four tasks, the RDS primary and standby, two Redis nodes, and in Singapore a load balancer, one task, the read replica and a small Redis node. Small fixed charges also continue: the WAF web ACL, the secret, the KMS key and the hosted zone. Lambda, SQS, CloudFront and Route 53 queries cost almost nothing without traffic. S3 charges for what is stored.

      A sale brings ten times the traffic. What grows by itself, and what must you prepare?

      CloudFront and the load balancer grow by themselves. The ECS service adds tasks when its scaling policy sees the load. Orders pile up in the queue and Lambda runs more copies of the worker. The RDS primary is the limit, because every write goes to one instance: raise the cache hit rate, add read replicas, and move to a larger instance before the sale, not during it.

      How much time and data do you lose when an AZ fails? And when the Region fails?

      An AZ: one to two minutes of failed writes while RDS fails over, and no data, because the standby is synchronous. The Region: minutes, for health checks to fail, DNS answers to expire, the replica to be promoted and the app tier to scale out; and the last few seconds of writes, because cross-Region replication is asynchronous. The time is the RTO and the data is the RPO.

      The company wants a smaller bill. What do you remove first, and what do you give up?

      Start with the second Region. Turn warm standby into pilot light: keep the read replica and the S3 copies, but run no tasks until a disaster. Recovery then takes longer, because the app tier must start from nothing. Do not remove the Multi-AZ standby or the second NAT Gateway: an AZ failure is far more likely than a Region failure.

      Is anything in this drawing still a single point of failure?

      No single AZ, and no single Region. What remains is not infrastructure. A bad deployment reaches every task, and a wrong DELETE is replicated to every copy, so you still need staged rollouts, backups and point-in-time recovery. The database promotion in Singapore is a manual step, so it fails if nobody has practised it.
