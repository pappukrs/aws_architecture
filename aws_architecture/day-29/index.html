Multi-Account Setup

Every drawing so far sat inside one AWS account. A real company does not work that way. It has one account for production, one for testing, one for logs, and often hundreds more. An account is the strongest wall AWS offers: nothing crosses it unless you allow it. Today you draw how the accounts are arranged in a tree, how rules flow down that tree, and where the log archive account from Day 26 lives.

The diagram

Full picture: one organisation holds every account. The management account is at the top, attached straight to the root and outside every OU. Below the root, organisational units (OUs) group the accounts that need the same rules. SCPs, in red, are attached to the root and to OUs, and every account below inherits them. Each account has one job: security tools, logs, the network, shared tools, one stage of the application, or experiments. Control Tower sets up the base of this layout and IAM Identity Center gives people one sign-in for all of it. Lines in the tree mean “contains”. The dashed arrow is log delivery.

Concepts

AWS Organizations

  • An AWS account is a container for resources. It has its own users and roles, its own limits and its own bill.
  • AWS Organizations joins many accounts into one organisation. The account that creates it is the management account. All the others are member accounts.
  • Consolidated billing: one bill for all accounts, paid by the management account, and still broken down per account. Usage is added together, so volume discounts are reached sooner.
  • New accounts are created with one API call. Existing accounts can be invited.

Root and OUs

  • The root is the top of the tree. An organisation has exactly one. It is not the same thing as an account's root user.
  • An organisational unit (OU) is a folder for accounts. An OU can contain other OUs.
  • An account sits in one place in the tree at a time. You can move it later.
  • Group accounts by the rules they need, not by the company's departments. Security, Infrastructure, Workloads and Sandbox are a common starting set.
  • Anything attached to an OU applies to every account under it, including accounts added later.

Service control policies

  • An SCP is a policy attached to the root, to an OU or to one account. It sets the most that anyone in those accounts can do.
  • It never grants a permission. Users and roles still need an IAM policy, as on Day 25.
  • It binds every user and role in a member account, including the root user.
  • It does not apply to the management account. It also does not apply to service-linked roles, the roles AWS services use for themselves.
  • A Deny at any level wins. And an action must be allowed at every level, which is why FullAWSAccess is attached everywhere by default.

Security and logging accounts

  • Log Archive holds the central bucket from Day 26. The organisation-wide CloudTrail trail and AWS Config deliver there.
  • Audit, also called Security Tooling, is where the security team works. GuardDuty and Security Hub send findings from every account to it.
  • Both are separate from the workloads, so the people being watched cannot change what watches them.
  • Almost nobody can sign in to them, and their OU carries its own stricter SCP.

Control Tower

  • A landing zone is a multi-account environment that is ready and secure before the first workload arrives: the tree, the security accounts, central logging and sign-in.
  • Control Tower builds one on top of Organizations. It creates the Security OU with the Log Archive and Audit accounts. Newer Control Tower versions call these two accounts CloudTrail administrator and Config aggregator, and keep the Config logs in a bucket in the second one.
  • A guardrail, which AWS now calls a control, is a rule applied to a whole OU. A preventive one is an SCP: it stops the action. A detective one is an AWS Config rule: it reports what already breaks the rule. A third kind, proactive, checks a CloudFormation template before the resource is created.
  • Account Factory creates new accounts from a template, already in an OU, with logging and guardrails switched on.

IAM Identity Center

  • One place where people sign in, once, for every account. It was called AWS SSO before 2022.
  • Users and groups come from its own directory or from the company's identity provider, such as Microsoft Entra ID or Okta.
  • A permission set is a bundle of IAM policies. Assign a group and a permission set to an account, and Identity Center creates the matching IAM role in that account.
  • People receive temporary credentials each time. There are no IAM users and no long-lived access keys to leak.

A way to remember: a VPC is a wall for packets. An account is a wall for everything.

How accounts still work together

If every account is a wall, something must be allowed through. There are four standard doors.

  • Cross-account role: a role in account B that trusts account A. Someone in A assumes it and receives temporary credentials for B. This is how the pipeline in Shared Services deploys to prod, and how the Audit account looks into the others.
  • Resource sharing: AWS Resource Access Manager (RAM) shares one resource, such as the Transit Gateway or a subnet, with other accounts or with a whole OU.
  • Organisation-wide services: one CloudTrail trail, GuardDuty and AWS Config are switched on for every account from one place. A member account cannot turn the organisation trail off.
  • Delegated administrator: the management account hands the running of a service, such as GuardDuty, to a member account, usually Audit. The security team then never needs to sign in to the management account.

Check yourself

Why separate accounts instead of separate VPCs?

A VPC separates only the network. Inside one account, IAM users and roles, service quotas and the bill are shared, so an admin, a mistake or a stolen credential in one VPC can reach the resources of the other. An account separates all of it, and nothing crosses unless you allow it. You can also put rules above an account with SCPs, which you cannot do for a VPC.

The Infrastructure OU has only FullAWSAccess. A read-only role in the Network account tries to delete the Transit Gateway. Does it work?

No. An SCP never grants a permission. FullAWSAccess only means that the SCPs block nothing. The permission itself must come from the role's IAM policy, and a read-only policy does not allow deleting.

An SCP on the root denies stopping CloudTrail. An admin in the management account stops it anyway. How?

SCPs do not apply to the management account, not even those attached to the root. That is why the management account holds no workloads and why very few people can sign in to it.

Can the root user of a member account get around an SCP?

No. An SCP binds every user and role in a member account, and the root user too. No IAM policy can restrict a root user, but an SCP does.

You replace FullAWSAccess on an OU with an SCP that allows only EC2 and S3. What happens to Lambda in the accounts below?

It is blocked. An action must be allowed by an SCP at every level: the root, each OU on the way down, and the account. If one level has no SCP that allows Lambda, nobody in those accounts can use it, whatever their IAM policy says.

The Workloads OU denies creating IAM users. How do engineers sign in to the prod account?

Through IAM Identity Center. They sign in once, choose the account and a permission set, and receive temporary credentials for a role in that account. No IAM user and no stored access key is needed.

Why is the Log Archive account separate, and in its own OU?

So that whoever takes over or misuses a workload account cannot delete the record of what they did. Nobody does daily work in the Log Archive account, and its OU carries an SCP that forbids deleting the log buckets or the logs in them, even for an admin of that account.

What is a landing zone, and what does Control Tower do with it?

A landing zone is a multi-account environment that is ready and secure before any workload arrives. Control Tower builds it: the organisation, the Security OU with the Log Archive and Audit accounts, central logging, sign-in through Identity Center, and a set of guardrails.

Preventive guardrail or detective guardrail: what is the difference?

A preventive guardrail is an SCP, so the action fails when someone tries it. A detective guardrail is an AWS Config rule, so the action succeeds and the resource is then reported as non-compliant. Use the first for things that must never happen, and the second for things you want to see and fix.

A new team needs an account tomorrow. How do you make sure it is safe from the first minute?

Create it with Account Factory, into the right OU. The account then inherits that OU's SCPs at once, its logs already go to the Log Archive account, and people reach it through Identity Center. Nobody has to remember to switch these on.
